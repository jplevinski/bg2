Chapter One

Tetherpoint Academy

The gates of Tetherpoint AcademyLocation — AcademyThe premier adventurer academy in Valerguard, founded during the Age of Ashes.→ Read moreTetherpoint Academy were taller than the granary back home, and I had spent eleven years imagining them wrong.

In my head they'd been gold. They were black iron, older than the kingdom, and the sun off the spires behind them hit my eyes like a blessing I hadn't asked permission for. My heart was going hard enough that I checked, stupidly, whether the people around me could hear it.

I made it. The thought arrived plain, no trumpets. Every candle I'd burned to a stub over borrowed books. Every winter dawn mucking stalls before lessons. Every time my father looked at my marks and nodded once, which was his whole vocabulary for pride. All of it had been for this gate, and now the gate was in front of me, and I couldn't move.

Smith-hammers rang on trainee steel. A merchant shoved a glowing manual at my chest and quoted a price that would've bought two of my father's bulls. An actual adventurer walked past — cloak, scars, a sword with a name, probably — and nobody stared but me.

Six years. Keep my head down, keep my marks up, don't get expelled, and this was home.

It was a long way from RavenscroftLocation — VillageElias's ancient home village. Rarely does anyone from here attend Tetherpoint.→ Read moreRavenscroft. A few thousand souls, cattle and soil, a village so old nobody remembered founding it. My father ran one of the bigger stocks and worked a forge on the side. My mother had studied once, at a small academy for agriculture and community service — that's where they met, before Tether spat him back out to the fields. Simple folk. People said it like a diagnosis.

I was the first in generations to stand at this gate.

Well. The first anyone would admit to.

There had been a great-uncle. Mother's side. Once the family's shining pride — I knew that much only because of how thoroughly he'd been erased. Necessary. Dangerous. Both words got used. Nobody ever explained how both could be true.

"Time to get your stats checked out."

And despite everything — the nerves, the noise, the strangeness — a spark went through me, because that was the promise. The shove nearly put me on the cobbles. I scrambled forward, gathering what dignity I had left, which fit comfortably in one hand.

Ahead, a stone arch was built into the base of a tower, and where a door should have been there was a wound in the world — purple, black, and crimson, churning like water deciding whether to drown you.

A portal. The The Academy GateMagical StructureA portal arch that transforms those who pass through into adventurers.→ Read moreAcademy Gate.

Elias and Kip before the Academy Gate

I'd read about portals my whole life. Some crossed continents. Some sealed ruins that were better left sealed. This one made adventurers, and standing in front of it, my chest felt like a cage full of birds that had just understood what cages were.

A short red-haired boy stood frozen at the threshold ahead of me. His turn came and he simply... stopped.

"I— I don't know if I—"

"Oh, look." The voice came from behind me, pitched to carry. "Baby's scared."

The speaker shouldered forward — big, and built like he knew it, with the specific grin of someone who'd figured out early that size plus cruelty equaled getting whatever he wanted, and had never needed a second lesson.

"Maybe we should send him back to his mama."

The red-haired kid folded inward, shoulders curling like he was trying to be absorbed by his own robes.

Here is a thing that has always been true about me, and would eventually be the ruin of me: I have never once feared a consequence as much as I've hated a bully.

"Leave him alone."

The big one turned. The grin widened into something with more teeth in it. "And who the fuck are you?"

Nobody. Elias from Ravenscroft, first day, no stats, no friends, no plan.

"You heard me," I said.

The fist arrived before the sentence finished being brave.

The world spun once, generously showing me sky, tower, cobblestones. Then the cobblestones came up to meet my back and three of them had my arms pinned. The big one planted his full weight on my face like he was claiming territory.

"Thought you'd play hero?"

What followed was a sulfuric act of war that I will not dignify with description, except to say that his friends howled, and I gagged, and some small ledger in the back of my skull opened to a fresh page and wrote his face on it.

"Get off me, you oversized pig!" — muffled, from a position I'd rather history forgot.

"Hero's got spirit!"

"Let's see how brave he is inside!"

Hands hauled me up. The red-haired boy screamed. And before either of us could do a single thing about it, they threw us into the portal.

That mouth-breathing bastard and his pack. I wouldn't forget them.

It turned out I was right about that. Just not in any way I could have survived knowing.

Chapter Two

The Weight of a Name

The portal spat us into a stone hall big enough to make everything in Ravenscroft retroactively embarrassing. Rows of chairs faced a raised podium. Banners bearing the Tetherpoint crest hung from a vaulted ceiling that had clearly never once worried about the price of candles.

All around me, my new classmates were comparing revelations.

"My Strength is twelve!"

"I got a Fire Affinity!"

"No way — show me, show me—"

The energy was infectious, if you were in the mood for a pep rally. I was in the mood to stare at the edge of my vision, where a line of silver text had appeared, said something meant for me, and unsaid it.

"I saw your screen."

The red-haired kid from the portal was suddenly at my elbow, delivering this the way another person might report the weather. "I do not have Legacy Features."

I blinked at him. "You what?"

"Legacy Features," he repeated, patiently, like I was the strange one. "Mine does not have them."

A knot pulled tight in my gut. I hadn't even been sure the words were real — half a second of flicker, then gone. Now they had a name, and the name had a witness. I changed the subject before my thoughts could spiral somewhere useful.

"Name's Elias. Elias Lumina." I stuck out my hand.

He stared at it long enough for me to reconsider the custom of handshakes as a species, then seized it in both of his and pumped like he was drawing well water. "Kip Krumplnick," he announced.

"Krumplnick."

"That is my surname."

"Right."

He kept looking at me — not rudely, just totally, the way you'd study a page you intended to memorize. It was deeply unsettling and, I'd learn, permanent.

"I noticed," he continued, "that you do not have an Affinity yet. I do not either."

I shrugged. "Guess we're both unlucky."

Kip lit up like I'd handed him a festival prize. "I am delighted."

There is no correct response to that sentence. I didn't find one.

A snort carried across the hall. The oversized bastard from the gate stood with his pack, pointing at me, rubbing his knee in exaggerated pantomime — look, the farm boy fell — and howling. His friends howled along, because that's what a pack is for.

I glared with everything I had. Spontaneous combustion remained, sadly, outside my skill tree.

"Why did you intervene in my social interaction with Cassian?" Kip asked.

I turned to him slowly. "Your social interaction."

"Yes."

"The part where he punched me, pinned me down, and farted on my face?"

Kip nodded, solemn as a judge. "Yes."

"You know he's a bully, right?"

Kip frowned — and here's the thing, it was genuine confusion, root to branch. "No. Cassian Drake is my friend."

I laughed before I understood he meant it. "Your friend."

"Oh yes. We have known each other since we were six. We wrestle often."

I was assembling an explanation of the difference between wrestling and getting beaten by someone twice your size when Kip's whole face went bright. "Cassian!" And he trotted straight over. Trotted. Like a dog hearing its dish.

Cassian groaned for his audience. "Oh gods, the goblin found me."

Kip sailed through the insult without touching it. "Can I see your stats screen?"

Cassian took him by the collar, folded him into a headlock, and ground knuckles into his scalp. "Why are you so damn weird?" he laughed, and the pack laughed, and Kip winced in real pain and did not fight back. Didn't even look angry. When Cassian shoved him loose, Kip straightened his clothes, fixed his hair, and resumed asking about Strength scores as though the previous fifteen seconds had happened to somebody else.

I watched the whole display in silence.

Kip thought Cassian was his friend. What I saw was a boy who'd been trained — carefully, over years — to receive cruelty and call it play. And for reasons I couldn't have put words to yet, I hated Cassian Drake a little more than I had five minutes ago.

The ledger in the back of my skull added a line. I didn't hear the quill. You never do.

"Welcome! Welcome, Class 777 — The Golden CohortStudent BodyElias's incoming class, called "the luckiest of numbers" by Archmage Varric.→ Read moreClass 777!"

The voice owned the hall before I found its source: a wizard at the podium in crimson-and-gold robes, actual flames stitching themselves along the hems like living embroidery. Fifty-ish face, twenty-ish voice. Dark hair shot with silver. Eyes swirling blue-white, like storms kept as pets.

"What we have come to call — the Golden Cohort!"

"You are our hope," he declared, arms wide enough to embrace the architecture. "Our dreams. Triple sevens! The luckiest of numbers! Fortune smiles upon you this day, and history shall remember you as the brightest generation Tetherpoint Academy has ever produced."

The room detonated. Kids leapt up, hugged strangers, shouted their futures at each other. And I sat there, damp with humiliation, doing the arithmetic of my first day: punched, pinned, farted on, and shown a set of secret stats that existed, as far as I could tell, so something could keep score of my soul.

Luck wasn't the word I'd have picked.

I was just Elias Lumina. Farm kid. Ordinary parents. No prophecy, no title, no—

The wizard's eyes found me across three hundred heads. It felt like being stepped on.

"Interesting," he said, and the warmth left his voice like heat leaving a corpse. "It appears we have one student in the back scowling during my introduction."

Laughter rippled. My face went furnace-hot. I glanced around on the desperate chance he meant some other scowling farm boy. The hall's geometry disagreed.

He took up a long black staff — its head a crystal where blue ice and red flame circled each other like old enemies — and came down from the podium. The hall went quieter with each step. When he stopped in front of me, those storm-lit eyes moved over my face the way a locksmith's hands move over a lock.

Then something behind them turned.

"Oh," he said, very softly. "I see."

"...What?"

"What is your name, boy."

"Elias."

"Your surname."

"Lumina."

The color left his face all at once, the way it does when a man sees not a stranger but a ghost wearing one. His jaw set. And then — in front of three hundred students, in the middle of his own welcome speech — he spat. The glob struck stone an inch from my boot.

The hall went silent as a held breath.

"What the hell, sir?" It was out of me before wisdom could vote.

The whispers came up like floodwater. Did he say Lumina? — I thought that family was finished — no wonder—

No wonder what?

The wizard drew himself straight, composure sliding back over his face like a visor, though nothing warm came back with it. "Yes," he murmured, mostly to himself. "A Lumina. Of course."

I'd braced for nobles sneering at the farm kid. Every kid from a village braces for that. I had not braced for one of the most powerful men in the academy to look at my name like it was something he'd scraped off his boot — and to be afraid while he did it.

"Get to your seat, novice."

I wanted answers. I wanted them loudly. But there was a stillness in his eyes of the specific kind that precedes regrettable events, and even a farm boy knows not to slap the bull. I sat.

He strutted back to the podium as though he hadn't just publicly declared war on a fourteen-year-old.

"This is indeed a happy moment," he proclaimed. "I am Archmage Varric Pyre — Elementalist Extraordinary, master of ice and fire, veteran of many wars, and now your prestigious instructor at Tetherpoint Academy."

Applause thundered. I barely heard it. I was busy being fourteen and rearranging my entire understanding of my family. My great-uncle hadn't just embarrassed the Luminas. Whatever he'd done, it was still radioactive two generations later — hot enough to make an archmage spit at a child.

What did you do, old man?

Varric lectured on: eight hundred glorious years since the The Age of AshesHistorical EraAn ancient period during which Tetherpoint Academy was founded.→ Read moreAge of Ashes, legendary graduates, slain dragons, sealed rifts. Every few minutes he punctuated himself with fireworks — flame twisted into duelists, ice bloomed into spires — the magical equivalent of a man flexing in every mirror he passes.

"Look around you," he boomed at last, and the hall hushed. "Look left. Look right. Look at the poor souls beside and behind you."

I looked. Cassian sat ahead, wearing the smirk of a boy who has never once been told no by anyone who could enforce it. Kip stared at the ceiling, lips moving through some private mathematics.

"Statistics tell us only half of you will graduate."

Silence, sudden and total.

"Half will fail. Some will quit. Some will discover they were simply never meant for this." He let the fear breathe. Then he grinned. "And I say — nay to those statistics!"

His hands went up. Fire roared to the ceiling and spread into a phoenix with a wingspan wider than my father's barn. Gasps. Cheers. A flick of his fingers and the flames cooled, crystallized, and came down as snow — soft, glittering, ceremonial.

The students lost their minds. Fists in the air. Golden Cohort! Golden Cohort! And for one stupid, shining second, I almost let myself believe it. Maybe we were special. Maybe I had made it. Maybe—

SPLOOSH!

The avalanche hit me like a judgment. Not flakes. Slush — a full winter's worth, dumped square on my head, pouring ice down my collar and soaking me to the skin. I shot up out of my seat, gasping, scrubbing my eyes, and took inventory of the hall through the dripping: three hundred students dusted in pretty snow.

One student buried in it.

"Seriously — what the fuck?" My voice rang off the vaulted stone.

The laughter died. Every eye in the hall turned.

Varric rotated toward me with theatrical slowness. Whatever counterfeit warmth the speech had held was gone; what remained looked at me, looked at the slush, looked back at me. He knew I hadn't done a thing.

He simply didn't care. And he wanted me to see that he didn't. That was the lesson, I understood later. The snow was just the handwriting.

"I warned you, young Lumina," he said, cold as the ice in my collar. "Do not disrupt this gathering."

"But you—"

And here — I don't fully know how to explain what happened next, except that I raised my hand, and for one split second I could see it: the staff wrenched from his grip, the arc of it, the crack of crystal against his skull, the shock breaking open that smug storm-lit face. I could feel the weight of it in my palm like memory. Something in my chest pulled taut, and just underneath my ribs there was — pressure. Heat. Power, close enough to touch, if I only wanted it badly enough.

I wanted it.

Nothing happened.

Varric didn't so much as blink. "I did not ask for your explanation," he said, in a voice like a door being closed from the other side. "I asked for your silence."

The stubborn idiot who lives in my mouth opened it anyway—

Varric leveled the staff at my chest.

No flash. No thunder. One instant I was standing in the Great Hall, soaked and furious in front of the Golden Cohort. The next, I was falling through dark.

Stone met me. The air went out of my lungs in one flat cough.

I lay there a moment, then sat up, groaning, and took in my new accommodations: a small dreary room, bare walls, a cot with ambitions of being a bed, a slit window, a desk. Thick iron bars on the door. A brass plaque beside them, polished by generations of misfortune.

DISCIPLINE BUILDING — DETENTION CELL 4

I stared at it.

"Well," I said, to no one. "Fuck."

I dropped into the chair and put my head in my hands. Snow melted out of my hair and ticked onto the desk, one drop at a time, like a clock that only counted losses.

What was so poisonous about my name that an archmage would spit at the sound of it? Why had he looked afraid? Why the whispers — that family, no wonder — like everyone in the kingdom had read a page of my story that had been torn out of my copy?

I had worked for this my whole life. The late nights. The volunteer rolls. The perfect marks that made my father nod his single nod. Years of dreaming about black iron gates. Had I really climbed all that way just to burn down on day one — for a name I couldn't even get anyone to explain?

The cell offered no answers. Cells never do. That's rather the point of them.

◇ ◇ ◇

My thoughts were circling somewhere dark when the screech split them open.

At the slit window perched the strangest owl I had ever seen — ancient, feathers ragged as an old battle-standard, and its eyes wrong: one pitch black, drinking the light; the other burning gold, like it had seen too much and started keeping some. It studied me with an attention no bird should own.

We regarded each other for a long moment, the owl and I.

Then it hopped inside, landed on the desk with a thud far too heavy for its body, and stared some more — searching my face for something. Whatever the test was, I failed it. The owl huffed, an unmistakably disappointed sound, spread its wings, and was gone through the slit.

It left something behind.

A weathered envelope lay on the desk where, seconds ago, there had been nothing but melted snow. Yellowed. Soft with age, as if it had been carried a long time before it was sent. On the front, in silver ink:

To: Elias Lumina
From: Uncle Daemeon

I picked it up. Set it down. Picked it up again.

Nobody talked about Uncle Daemeon. Not once, not ever. His name was conversational poison — the kind that killed a room and made every adult in it check the door. I'd stopped asking about him years ago, the way you stop pressing a bruise.

Now, stuck in detention on day one — snow in my hair, my name a curse, an archmage's spit still drying on the stone of the Great Hall — I finally held something from the family ghost.

This time, I didn't put the envelope down.

The Story of Glintjaw — Chapter One

The Incubation of Greed

Era: 4 B.C.A. (Before the Containment Architecture)

Location: Bedlam Box — The Eastern Incubation Vaults

The world was younger then, raw and unlettered. It was a time before the Wizards Emporium drew its lines upon the earth, before there were laws to bind monsters or walls to keep the dark apart from the light. Before anyone was foolish enough to believe the world could ever be made safe.

Maelora did not care for safety. She was the God of Chaos, and when the world grew quiet, she took a strange delight in making things that screamed. She had filled the hollow places of the earth with beasts simply to watch them move. Some burned like tallow; some spilled poison into the stone; some tore the roots of mountains apart. Most died quickly, but failure never troubled her. A dead thing was only an experiment that had found its end.

Deep beneath the oldest peaks lay the Bedlam Box. The place breathed with a slow, damp heat. Stone sweated underfoot, and the walls shifted like a sleeping dog. High above, thousands of living sacs hung from the dark ceiling, heavy and ripe, each waiting to drop some new horror into the world.

Maelora walked among them, her boots clicking against the wet rock. Most of what she saw disappointed her — they were too large, too loud, or too simple. But then she stopped before a small, recessed vault. Inside sat a mimic.

It was a pathetic thing to look at. An ordinary chest made of cheap, splitting pine with rusted iron hinges. There was nothing remarkable about it, save for a single dull crystal where its lock should have been. It did not move, but the crystal watched her with a flat, mindless persistence.

She sighed, the sound echoing hollowly through the vault. "Pathetic," she murmured.

The chest snapped its lid shut, a sudden, petulant sound.

Maelora raised her hand, and the great wheel of affinity turned behind her eyes. Fire, shadow, death, and blood; the cold sting of the storm and the long, slow ruin of madness. Thousands of terrible possibilities spun past, a blur of dark color, before the wheel settled on something small and strange.

Affinity Assigned: Magical Items

She frowned. Then, slowly, her mouth curved. "Well," she whispered.

The mimic did not care about the wheel. Its single crystal eye was fixed entirely on an enchanted trinket hanging from the god's sleeve — a bright, dancing bit of glass. The air between them bent. With a sharp snap, the ornament tore free from her robes and flew straight into the chest's open mouth.

The little creature clamped down with a solid wood-on-wood thud, holding the prize tight as if the world might try to steal it back.

Maelora stared at her bare sleeve. "You little thief," she said.

Then she laughed. It was a sound like breaking ice, loud enough to set the mountain shaking. In the deeper tunnels, greater demons heard it and fell to their knees in the muck, trembling. But the mimic never looked away from her, its lid pulled tight over its stolen bit of glass.

"So that's what you are," she said, crouching down until her face was level with the splitting wood. "Glintjaw."

The name stayed with him. Almost at once, a row of dull gold appeared across his back teeth — worthless little caps, but they shone brighter than they had any right to in the dark. The mimic rattled, a sound like a handful of copper coins spilling across an old oak table. He loved the bright weight of treasure more than meat, more than life itself.

Maelora rose to her full height. "Go hoard," she commanded.

She turned to leave the vault, but a small, rhythmic tapping followed her. When she looked back, she found the mimic bouncing awkwardly across the stone floor after her crystal mallet. The hammer was nothing special to her — just a common tool she used to shape monster cores — but to Glintjaw, it was the center of the world.

He leaped, missed, and hit the stone with a dull clatter. He scrambled back onto his short legs, jumped again, and missed again.

The god watched him fail until her curiosity got the better of her. She lowered the mallet until the heavy crystal head touched the floor. Glintjaw seized it instantly, wrapping every stubby leg and both sides of his iron chassis around the handle. He pulled with everything he had, his pine body groaning with the effort.

The hammer did not budge. Maelora reached down and plucked it back from him. "No," she said.

The mimic froze. Then, with a sudden, vicious snap, he bit her. His small, golden teeth sank deep into the finger of the God of Chaos.

A terrible silence spread through the Eastern Vaults. No creature, great or small, had ever dared such a thing. Maelora looked down at her bleeding finger, and then she laughed harder than before, a sound that threatened to bring down the ceiling.

"You fearless little bastard," she said, lifting him up by his jaws to study him. He was tiny, weak, and absurdly greedy, but he had no fear in him at all.

She offered him the mallet a second time, and this time she let him keep it. Glintjaw wrapped his limbs around the crystal head like a drowning man clinging to an oar, refusing to let go.

With her other hand, Maelora tore a gray rift in the air. Beyond the tear lay a forgotten cave, cold and damp, where travelers sometimes wandered in search of easy riches. She tossed him through the gap, a small block of wood with a god's hammer locked tight against his chest.

"Wait there, little Glintjaw," she smiled as the rift began to close. "One day someone richer than you will come."

The portal sealed with a soft sigh of wind. She did not know, and could not guess, that she had just let loose the greediest creature in creation.

Glintjaw settled into the cold, quiet dark with his first treasure, but even then, he knew it would never be enough.

The Story of Glintjaw — Chapter Four

The Core Fixation

For six days the cavern lay in a silence that had weight to it, the kind of silence that comes before something, not after. Glintjaw did not like it.

He dragged his splintered frame through the muck on six stubby legs that did not yet move as one. He had grown wiser in his exile, and wisdom had taught him this much: he was being cheated. From the high tunnels came the voices of men, carried down on currents of cold air the way all sound traveled in that mountain, faint and strange. They spoke of disappearances. They spoke of a shaman who would not wake again. Fear had made them cautious, and caution kept them to the safe paths, far from his reach.

This did not sit well with him. He had no use for their fear, only for the bright metal they wore and the rings that hummed faintly with old power, the kind of treasure that made a wooden thing feel, for a moment, like something more than wood. But the bearers of such treasure no longer came his way.

So it fell to the old hunger to decide his course. That is the way of it, when one is a monster. One eats, or one does not.

◇ ◇ ◇

He felt the slimes before he found them—a faint vibration through the stone, the only sense a mimic truly trusts, for he had no nose to catch a scent on and no lungs to draw breath through. Three of them moved along the crystal vein in a slow procession, blind and earless things, green and glistening, going nowhere in particular with great purpose.

Glintjaw flattened himself against the cold floor. Wood does not breathe, and a thing that does not breathe can be very patient. He let his lid hang open the width of a finger, the way driftwood gapes after the river has finished with it and moved on to other business.

The lead slime came up close and pressed its formless face against his hinge.

He held still.

It pressed again, leaving behind a smear of something sour and faintly rotten.

Then his jaw came open.

It happened the way such things happen, swift after a long patience. He caught the first across its middle. The other two scattered, after their fashion, which was not scattering so much as a slow and foolish wobbling in two directions. He went left. That one tried the wall, and walls, as it turned out, do not answer the prayers of slimes; it slid back down into his waiting mouth as if delivered there.

The third he ran down properly, though properly is a generous word for any pursuit involving a slime. It left a shining trail on the stone, and twice he lost his footing chasing it, his new legs unlearned in such matters. He found the rhythm by the third stride—four legs for purchase, two trailing for balance, the way a young hound has not yet made peace with its own tail. He ran the creature into a dead end of stone, where it pulsed once against the rock as if it understood, in whatever dim way a slime understands anything, that the road had ended.

That one he ate slowly, and savored it.

The first two burned on the way down, a low and climbing heat that filled the dark cavity where his stomach ought to have been but was not, for he had something stranger in its place, a void-cavity that simply made things disappear into purpose. He swallowed through the burning, as he always did. When it passed, something in him had been remade, quietly, the way a key turns at last in a lock long given up for rusted shut.

He worked his jaw and tasted the last of it on whatever served him for a tongue. Some new chamber had opened in him to hold it.

The needleclaws were another matter entirely.

He came upon their nest by misfortune more than craft, a fissure choked with old webbing where dozens of them lay packed close, legs folded like switchblades against their black and segmented bodies. He set a foot down wrong, and the whole nest woke at once, as one creature with many wills.

They did not scatter as the slimes had scattered. They had the bearing of soldiers who had done this before. They came around him in a ring, cutting off every path before he had even marked the first sting. He snapped at the nearest and found only air. Three more ran their blade-legs down a seam in his shell while his jaw was still closing on nothing.

The pain came bright and many, not the long dull ruin the lightning would bring him later, but a hundred small and precise hurts opening together in the grain of his wood. He made a sound meant to be a roar that came out instead as splitting timber. He struck out in every direction and struck nothing.

There was a pattern in it, and he felt the shape of the pattern before he understood it. Some came low, for the joints of his legs. Some dropped from above, hunting the hinge of his lid. A third group held back and watched, patient as judges, waiting for him to commit his weight one way so the rest might open him from the other.

He stopped trying to bite all of them at once.

He chose one—a quick one, plainly the one the others followed, circling his back left leg with the easy confidence of long practice. He let two more cuts land while he watched only this one. He set the pain aside, the way a man sets aside a debt he intends to collect on later. When the point-runner finally committed, he struck for the place it occupied.

It was not there when his jaw arrived.

He struck again. It was not there a second time either.

The third time he did not aim for where the creature was, nor even for where it was going, but for where it would believe he must strike next, gambling that two failures had taught it to expect a third in the same vein. The gamble held. His jaw closed on the place its own leg carried it, faster than the small mind behind that leg could revise its plan.

He crushed it between his teeth.

What remained had no one left to follow, and their order broke apart into something honest and small—plain fear, every creature now only for itself. That, he understood completely. He stopped thinking and gave himself over to the work of claw and jaw and the sheer ruined weight of his own body, until the fissure ran black with paste and fell quiet once more. He came to know their anatomy in the only way that mattered to him: by feel, by where the joints surrendered, by which parts of them screamed loudest before they stopped.

When it was finished, his shell looked like timber that had survived flood and fire in the same season. He counted four gouges deep enough to bare the iron banding beneath.

The Quadrant Faction System

Structural Mechanics & Governance

Country-wide decisions in Valerguard are made by vote between all four factions and the Wizards Emporium — five bodies, each casting one vote. A decision requires a 4/5 supermajority to pass, ensuring no single faction can dominate national policy. Each faction uses its own internal system to decide how to cast its vote. Local decisions, by contrast, are far less formal — they fall to the most powerful adventurers in the area, whose word carries the weight of law by sheer force of capability.

No single faction possesses an inherent moral alignment; each functions as a structural necessity to maintain societal stability. Systemic instability occurs exclusively when one faction achieves ideological or military hegemony, disrupting the balance of power. This vulnerability is linked to the distinct mechanisms of enforcement utilized by each quadrant:

1. The Shield

Governance: Strict Military Hierarchy · Primary Mechanism: Violent Societal Conformity

Commanded by the High Warden with absolute authority, The Shield enforces the status quo through rigid top-down hierarchy. Wardens carry out directives without question, and ranks below follow suit. Public order is maintained through the aggressive suppression of non-conformity — behavior, lifestyles, or ideologies that deviate from baseline norms are eliminated through institutionalized force.

2. The Flame

Governance: Representative Council of 13 · Primary Mechanism: Weaponized Instability

Governed by a council of 13 elected representatives — the most powerful among them chosen to reach consensus on faction-wide decisions. Each representative holds the right to push one unilateral decision per year, provided three others on the council support it. This structure breeds constant internal competition and political maneuvering, generating controlled chaos that prevents systemic stagnation.

3. The Grove

Governance: Council of Ten Elders · Primary Mechanism: Enforced Ideological Harmony

Led by ten Elders who think so alike they function as a single mind, The Grove eliminates internal friction by prohibiting dissent. Societal cohesion is maintained through total narrative consensus — their singular focus is preserving peace at any cost. Individuals presenting dissenting voices are systematically removed from the general populace and routed to specialized centers for compulsory ideological re-education.

4. The Veil

Governance: Democratic Lot-Casting · Primary Mechanism: Shadow Operations

Paradoxically, the most feared faction is also the most democratic. Every member of The Veil casts a lot — decisions are made by collective vote, not decree. Yet the methods those votes authorize are anything but gentle: assassinations, necromancy, demonology, and operations conducted entirely in shadow. The Shield despises them not for how they govern, but for how they fight — through secrecy and subterfuge rather than open confrontation.

The Lunarian Exodus

Classification: Ancient myth / disputed historical event Era: ~2,500 years before the Catastrophe

Overview

Approximately 2,500 years before the Catastrophe, a group of beings from Luna — the Lunarians — are said to have descended to Valerguard and surrendered their immortality to take on human form. They did not arrive as conquerors or missionaries. They arrived quietly, mixed with the mortal population, and over generations became indistinguishable from ordinary humans — except for certain gifts that persisted in the blood.

Most people in Valerguard have never heard of the Lunarian Exodus. It does not appear in academy-approved histories. It is not taught in any recognized institution. What little survives of it exists in fragmented cult records, suppressed genealogies, and the oral traditions of a handful of families who have largely stopped telling the story themselves.

Those who have heard of it know it primarily as the founding myth of the Cult of Luna — a creation story that members of that order used to explain their bloodlines, their magic, and their claim to a lunar inheritance. Most who encounter it in this context treat it the same way they treat all cult mythology: as a fairytale. A flattering origin story invented by a religious group to make itself feel chosen.

It is possible they are right. It is also possible they are not.

The Five Accounts

No single version of the Exodus survives intact. What remains are five competing accounts, each preserved by a different tradition, each offering a different reason why the Lunarians came — and why they gave up what they were.

I — The Exile

According to the oldest surviving fragment, the Lunarians did not choose to come. They were cast out — exiled from Luna by those who held power there, for a transgression that the records do not name. Mortality was not a gift they sought. It was the punishment levied against them: stripped of their deathlessness and sent to a world that would eventually consume them. Those who hold this account point to the Lunarians' quiet disappearance into the general population as evidence — not of peaceful integration, but of people who could not afford to be found.

II — The Bargain

A second account, preserved in Cult of Luna ceremonial texts, holds that the Exodus was a deliberate exchange. The Lunarians came to Valerguard to fulfill an agreement with a divine force — the nature and identity of the other party left deliberately vague in surviving records. In exchange for taking on mortality and anchoring themselves in the mortal world, they were permitted to bring something with them: knowledge, tools, or an artifact that would only work in mortal hands. What they brought, and why it required their transformation, is not explained.

III — The Flight

A third tradition holds that the Lunarians fled. Not exile — flight. Something happened on Luna that made remaining there impossible or unbearable, and Valerguard was simply the nearest viable destination. The transformation from immortal to mortal was not chosen for philosophical or diplomatic reasons; it was a necessary cost of crossing. Whatever method existed for a Lunarian to take on human form required surrendering the thing that made them Lunarian. They paid it because the alternative was worse. This account is the most troubling, because it implies that whatever drove them out may still exist.

IV — The Mercy

The most romanticized version, preserved in fragments of pre-Catastrophe poetry, describes the Lunarians as beings who watched the mortal world from above for ages and were undone by what they saw. Not repulsed — drawn. Valerguard was young and violent and finite, and to beings who had lived in perfect stillness since before memory, something in that brief, burning existence became unbearable to merely observe. They chose to descend. They chose to become mortal. They traded eternity for the ability to be present in a world that actually changed. This version is the one most often dismissed as a fairytale, because it is the most beautiful.

V — The Seeding

The fifth account is the least poetic and the most disturbing. It holds that the Lunarians did not come for themselves at all. They came because they were sent — not punished, not persuaded, but deployed. Some larger intelligence or governing body on Luna identified Valerguard as a world that would eventually require intervention, and the Lunarians were placed within its population to ensure that when the critical moment arrived, something with lunar knowledge and lunar capability would already be present. The transformation into mortals was not a cost. It was a feature. It made them undetectable. Whether this account implies the Lunarians were aware of their purpose, or simply instruments who never knew what they were, is unclear.

What They Left Behind

Regardless of which account is true — or whether any of them are — the descendants of the Lunarians carried something forward. Lunar Magic persisted in certain bloodlines long after its origins were forgotten. The ability to work with living crystal, to siphon and redirect magical energy, to anchor souls and resist corruption — these gifts did not emerge naturally in Valerguard. They were inherited.

The Cult of Luna formed, in part, around families who still carried these traits and recognized what they meant. They preserved the Exodus as their founding myth not because it was provable, but because it was the only explanation that fit what they were.

Most people who hear the story smile and move on. They have no reason to believe that immortal beings from the moon chose to become mortal and live quietly among them for thousands of years.

They are probably right.

Probably.

The Cult of Luna

Classification: Pre-Catastrophe lunar order Status: Extinct, suppressed, or absorbed into The Veil
Era: ~2,000 years before the Catastrophe Also known as: The Lunar Covenant · The Argent Forge · The Moon-Keepers

Overview

The Cult of Luna was one of the most misunderstood organizations of the pre-Catastrophe world. Modern histories describe it as a heretical moon-worshipping sect obsessed with forbidden rites, bloodline purity, death magic, and relic construction. That description is not entirely false, but it is deliberately incomplete.

The Cult of Luna was not merely a religion. It was a civilization within a civilization: a priesthood, forge order, warrior caste, bloodline archive, and magical research body built around the remnants of ancient Lunar Magic.

Most people in Valerguard have never heard of the Cult beyond vague academy warnings about dead heresies. Those who have heard of it usually dismiss its deeper myths as symbolic nonsense. The most controversial of those myths is the Lunarian Exodus: the claim that certain bloodlines did not originate in Valerguard at all, but came from Luna itself.

Few believe this literally. Fewer still know enough to be afraid of it.

Public Reputation

In the modern academy-approved version of history, the Cult of Luna was a dangerous pre-Catastrophe sect that practiced shadow rites, soul manipulation, corpse animation, lunar blood rituals, and forbidden artifact forging.

Its members were said to believe that the moon was not merely a celestial body, but a living source of divine power. They claimed certain families carried "moon-blood" and were therefore entitled to wield magic outside the accepted elemental and factional structures.

After the Catastrophe, surviving institutions reclassified nearly all lunar practices as dark magic. The Cult's records were destroyed, sealed, altered, or absorbed into restricted Veil archives.

The surviving public judgment is simple: The Cult of Luna reached too far, touched what should have remained dead, and helped bring ruin upon the world.

The truth is more complicated.

True Function

The Cult of Luna preserved fragments of a much older magical system: Lunar Magic.

Lunar Magic was broader than Shadow Magic. It governed reflection, absorption, concealment, gravity, tides, soul anchoring, resurrection, sealing, and the shaping of magical cores. Shadow Magic was only one branch of it — the branch most associated with absence, death, veiling, apparitions, and the boundary between life and what follows.

After the Catastrophe, early Veil members recovered only fragments of the larger system. Shadow Magic survived most fully because those records, rituals, and tools were the easiest to preserve or reconstruct. Other branches survived in damaged form: Siphoning, Reflection, Moonforging, and binding arts remained, but none as complete as Shadow.

This is why modern Veil magic appears darker than its origin. It is not the whole moon. It is the shadow left after the moon was forgotten.

The Lumina Connection

The Luminas were not ordinary members of the Cult of Luna. They belonged to its forge-ruler caste: warrior-smiths, lunar artificers, and sacred wardens entrusted with dangerous relics and living crystal. In old records, they are sometimes described as Argent Forge-Wardens, Moonforged Priests, or the Silver Hammer Line.

They were not passive priests. They were armed smiths with ritual authority. They could forge a weapon, bless it, wield it, and execute judgment with it.

Their magic was lunar in origin, though later histories would call it dark. The Luminas specialized in moonforging, core-shaping, reflection, absorption, and the binding of unstable magical forces. They were not merely users of Lunar Magic. They were makers.

This is why fragments of the Mallet of Maelora react specifically to Lumina blood. Other descendants of Moon-born people could wield branches of Lunar Magic. Some became shadow-workers. Some became binders. Some became seers, siphoners, or reflection mages. The Luminas were different. They were the line that forged the Mallet.

The Mallet of Maelora

The Cult's oldest myth claims that the Mallet of Maelora was not forged by Maelora herself. It was forged for her by a bloodline whose origin traced back to Luna.

Modern scholars usually dismiss this as priest-poetry — a symbolic way of saying the mallet was created under moonlight, or by a sect devoted to lunar rites. The Cult's own records are less symbolic.

They claim the Mallet was a divine core-shaping instrument, built to alter the structure of living magic. In Maelora's hands, it became a tool of monster creation. In Glintjaw's possession, it became something worse: a tool that learned hunger.

When the Mallet was shattered, its fragments were sealed away. Most were believed destroyed. Some were hidden. Some passed into forbidden collections. One eventually became the pendant inherited by Elias Lumina.

The fragment does not glow for Elias because he is powerful. It glows because it recognizes the hands that made it.

The Lunarian Exodus

The most forbidden belief associated with the Cult of Luna is the Lunarian Exodus.

According to this myth, the first Lunarians did not age and had mastered resurrection so completely that accidental death was considered reversible. They understood soul anchoring, body restoration, memory preservation, and the lunar cycles that governed return.

At some point in the ancient past, a group of Lunarians came to Valerguard and took on human life. Whether this was exile, mercy, punishment, experiment, or bargain remains unknown.

The Cult of Luna may have formed around their descendants. It may have preserved their rites. It may have protected the bloodlines that carried their gifts.

Most historians reject the Exodus as religious metaphor. The Veil does not officially confirm it. The Luminas themselves do not fully understand it.

But certain artifacts remember.

Magical Practices

The Cult of Luna practiced several branches of Lunar Magic.

Shadow Work
The most complete surviving branch. Used for concealment, apparitions, death-threshold contact, undead animation, fearcraft, and hidden movement.
Siphoning
The absorption and redirection of magical force. Rare, dangerous, and poorly understood after the Catastrophe.
Reflection
The art of returning, reversing, or altering incoming magic. Used in wards, mirrors, counterspells, and truth-reversal rites.
Moonforging
The crafting of lunar artifacts, living crystal, core-tools, binding instruments, and weapons that could hold or reshape magic.
Soul Anchoring
A degraded remnant of Lunarian resurrection arts. In its original form, it preserved identity and enabled return. In modern Shadow Magic, it survives as apparitions, bound spirits, undead vessels, and soul echoes.
Silver Binding
Sealing magic used to contain unstable entities, monster cores, forbidden relics, and dangerous magical principles.

The Luminas were not necessarily the rulers of the entire Cult, but they held a position no ordinary priest could claim. They were permitted to touch living crystal, shape lunar metal, and work directly with artifacts that could alter the structure of magic itself.

The Catastrophe and Suppression

After the Catastrophe, the Cult of Luna became a convenient corpse for history to blame.

Its rites were outlawed. Its records were burned. Its surviving members were hunted, absorbed, or driven underground. Many lunar-descended families disappeared into The Veil, where their broken traditions could survive under new names.

Shadow Magic became the dominant surviving form because the Veil had enough remaining material to reconstruct it. Other lunar branches became rare, unstable, or nearly extinct.

The word "cult" itself may be a post-Catastrophe insult. Older records suggest the organization called itself something closer to The Lunar Covenant or The Argent Order.

History remembers them as cultists because the winners needed them to be monsters.

Modern Relevance

In the present age, the Cult of Luna is considered dead. That is only partly true.

Its temples are gone. Its public priesthood is gone. Its forge-castes are gone. Its rites are fragmented. Its name survives mostly as a warning.

But The Veil still carries pieces of its magic. The Lumina bloodline still reacts to lunar relics. The Mallet fragments still remember their makers.

And if the Lunarian Exodus was real, then the Cult of Luna was never merely a religion.

It was a memory of home.

The Peoples of Valerguard

Races

The intelligent races of the known world — shaped by magic, catastrophe, and centuries of conflict.

Orcs

The orcs are among the youngest intelligent races in existence, appearing only after The Catastrophe shattered the world eight centuries ago.

Born within the magical wastelands surrounding The Maw, they emerged where immense concentrations of mana intertwined with countless human souls lost during the explosion. Flesh formed around spirit, creating a race unlike anything that had existed before.

Modern scholars believe orcs are simply another intelligent species born from magical evolution.

They are wrong.

No living being has yet discovered the truth.

The Secret Origin

Every orc carries the soul of a human who perished during The Catastrophe.

That knowledge was buried with the dead.

Physically, orcs are terrifying. Adult males average 6 feet 5 inches and nearly 300 pounds of dense muscle, while females average 6 feet and approximately 215 pounds. Their bones are thicker, their muscles denser, and their bodies naturally absorb and channel ambient mana far more efficiently than humans. An average orc can overpower an average human with ease.

Yet this tremendous strength came at a cost.

Unlike the older races, an orc's body was forged from the same chaotic mana that gives birth to monsters. Deep within every orc lingers an instinctive aggression—a persistent pressure urging decisive action, territoriality, and violence. Most learn to master these impulses through discipline, family, and culture, but no orc is ever completely free from them.

It is not hatred.

It is instinct.

The first orcs had little chance to prove otherwise.

When they wandered from the lands surrounding The Maw, they were met not with curiosity, but with fear. Towering figures infused with unstable magic appeared no different from the monsters already ravaging nearby settlements. Human militias hunted them relentlessly, believing they were preventing another invasion from the corrupted lands.

Few of those first orcs survived.

The survivors remembered.

Generation after generation inherited stories of humanity's blades, while humanity inherited stories of monstrous green giants emerging from the scar left by The Catastrophe.

Eight centuries later, both peoples remain prisoners of those first encounters.

The Greatest Irony

The race humanity fears most is, in spirit, humanity itself.

Every orc carries within them the soul of a human whose life ended on the day the world broke.

The Southern Migration

The first century of the New Era was one of constant bloodshed for the fledgling orc race. Small tribes wandered the lands surrounding The Maw, clashing repeatedly with human settlements that viewed them as little more than intelligent monsters. Entire clans disappeared before producing a second generation.

Everything changed around 100 N.E.

Orc scouts pushing farther south discovered a vast stretch of rugged territory beyond Valerguard. Human settlements were scarce, not because the land was unclaimed, but because few humans wished to live there. Steep mountains, rocky soil, and an unforgiving climate made large-scale agriculture nearly impossible.

To the orcs, it was ideal.

Separated from Valerguard by miles of dense wetlands, the region possessed a natural defensive barrier that discouraged large military campaigns while simultaneously providing abundant fresh water and hunting grounds. The marshes teemed with fish, amphibians, and waterfowl, while hardy swamp grasses could be cultivated to supplement their diet.

Beyond the wetlands, the mountain ranges supported enormous flying reptiles whose nests became prized hunting grounds for the strongest warriors. Along the southern coastline, rich fisheries ensured that even harsh winters rarely brought famine.

Unlike humans, whose civilizations depended upon fertile plains and grain production, orcs thrived as hunters, fishers, and gatherers. Their immense physical strength allowed them to exploit terrain that other races considered inhospitable, transforming an overlooked wilderness into their ancestral homeland.

Within only a few generations, wandering clans became permanent settlements.

Those settlements became villages.

The villages united into tribes.

And from those tribes emerged the first true Orcish civilization.

To this day, the wetlands remain both a lifeline and a shield, separating the Orcish Territories from Valerguard. Trade occasionally crosses the marshes, but so too do raiding parties, patrols, and old prejudices. Though open war has become rare, the natural frontier continues to mark the uneasy border between two peoples whose histories began in blood.

The Adventurer's System

Stats Guide

Every adventurer in Valerguard receives their stats through the Soul Enchantment performed at the Academy Gate. Core Stats govern physical and mental ability. Soul Balance Stats are hidden — most adventurers never know they exist.

Core Stats

Strength

"Resolute Fortitude" — Valerguard phrase for strength

Raw physical power. Determines melee damage, carrying capacity, and the ability to overpower opponents. Essential for warriors and frontline fighters.

Agility

Speed, reflexes, and coordination. Affects dodge chance, attack speed, and movement. Critical for scouts, rogues, and ranged combatants.

Vitality

Health, endurance, and resilience. Determines maximum HP, stamina recovery, and resistance to physical ailments. The foundation of survival.

Intelligence

Analytical ability and magical aptitude. Governs spell power, mana capacity, and the ability to learn complex techniques. The primary stat for mages and scholars.

Wisdom

Perception, intuition, and judgement. Affects mana regeneration, resistance to mental effects, and the ability to read situations. Valued by healers, strategists, and leaders.

Charisma

Force of personality, persuasion, and presence. Influences social interactions, leadership effectiveness, and certain enchantment-based abilities. Rare as a primary stat.

Soul Balance Stats (Hidden)

These stats are invisible to most adventurers. They shift based on lived experience — choices, trauma, relationships, and identity. Their true purpose within the System remains unknown.

Mercy

The capacity to forgive, empathize, and value the lives of others above one's own interests. High Mercy increases healing affinity, enhances support magic, and makes bonds easier to form. Low Mercy makes ruthlessness easier, sacrifices more acceptable, and may strengthen offensive abilities.

Conviction

The strength of one's beliefs under hardship. Measures how firmly a person holds to their principles when tested. High Conviction grants resistance to mental manipulation and strengthens resolve-based abilities. It rises when a person stands firm — and falls when they compromise what they believe.

Resentment

The accumulation of grief, anger, betrayal, and unresolved pain. Unlike other Soul Stats, Resentment is dangerous — it grows from injustice and suffering. High Resentment may unlock dark abilities but risks corrupting other Soul Balance stats. Why the System measures it remains a troubling question.

Connection

The depth of bonds with others. Reflects trust, loyalty, and emotional openness. High Connection strengthens party synergies, cooperative magic, and bond-based abilities. It drops when relationships fracture or isolation takes hold.

Hope

Belief that the future can be better than the present. Fuels resilience, recovery, and light-aligned magic. When Hope falls, despair creeps in — dulling willpower, slowing recovery, and opening the door to darker paths. Hope is fragile. Once broken, it is difficult to rebuild.

Identity

The degree to which a person remains true to their original self. Starts at its highest point and can only erode. Identity falls when a person is forced to become something they are not — through trauma, manipulation, or choices that betray who they once were. If Identity reaches zero, the person the System originally measured no longer exists.

The Adventurer's System

Battle Mechanics

The System governs every aspect of combat in Valerguard — from how resources are calculated to how damage is categorized and applied. Understanding these mechanics is the difference between surviving a fight and not.

Resource Formulas

HP — Hit Points

(STR + VIT × 2) × 3

Represents raw physical durability. Vitality carries the greater weight — endurance and resilience matter more than brute force when it comes to surviving damage. Strength contributes as a secondary factor, reflecting how muscle and fortitude work together.

MP — Mana Points

(WIS + INT × 2) × 3

Represents magical capacity. Intelligence is the dominant factor — it governs raw mana generation and spellcraft potential. Wisdom shapes how that mana is stored and regenerated, acting as a reservoir for what Intelligence produces.

Rage — Ravager Resource

Fixed pool — generated through combat

Rage is not derived from a stat formula. It is built exclusively through battle — taking hits, landing strikes, and witnessing allies fall. A Ravager begins each encounter at zero and must earn their resource through the fight itself. The pool maximum is set by class rank, not attribute scores.

• Adventurers with both a primary and secondary class may possess multiple resource types simultaneously. Elias Lumina is currently the only known example — his Tank (Ravager) primary class generates Rage, while the Siphon Mage secondary class unlocked a dormant MP pool.

• All formulas reflect the base calculation at the time stats are recorded. Equipment bonuses, temporary buffs, and class passives may alter the final displayed values.

• Soul Balance Stats do not feed into any known resource formula. Their mechanical effects on resource pools — if any — remain undocumented by the System.

Damage Types

All damage dealt in Valerguard falls into one of three categories. Resistances, vulnerabilities, and class abilities are indexed against these types — knowing what you're up against determines how you survive it.

⚔️ Melee

Physical damage delivered at close range. Governed primarily by Strength. Resisted by armor, shields, and Vitality-based passives.

🪨 Bludgeoned
Blunt impact — maces, hammers, fists. Bypasses light armor. Can stagger.
🗡️ Stab
Puncture damage — daggers, spears, rapiers. High critical potential. Finds gaps in plate.
⚔️ Slice
Cutting damage — swords, axes, claws. Causes Bleed on vulnerable targets.

✨ Magic

Elemental and arcane damage delivered through spellcraft. Governed by Intelligence. Resisted by Wisdom-based defenses, faction-specific wards, and certain legacy items.

🔥 Fire
Applies Burning. Deals damage over time. Countered by water resistance.
💧 Water
Applies Slow or Freeze. Reduces Agility. Amplifies Lightning damage when active.
🌿 Earth
Applies Root. Immobilizes targets. Effective against aerial or fast-moving enemies.
🌑 Shadow
Applies Fear or Blind. Drains Soul Balance stats. Favored by The Veil.
☀️ Light
Applies Stun or Reveal. Counters Shadow damage. Amplified by high Mercy.
🧪 Poison
Applies Poison. Deals damage over time and reduces healing received. Cannot be burned off.

🏹 Ranged

Physical damage delivered at distance. Governed by Agility. Resisted by cover, shields, and high Agility dodge chance.

🏹 Piercing
Arrows, bolts, thrown blades. High penetration against light armor. Causes Bleed on crits.
💥 Blunt Force
Thrown rocks, sling ammunition. Can stagger or knock back on impact.
💣 Explosive
Artificer grenades, volatile reagents, unstable compounds. Deals area-of-effect damage. Applies Burning or Shrapnel depending on composition. Friendly fire possible.

Notable Specimen

Glintjaw the Greedy
The Mimic That Ended the World

A baseline Mimic of unremarkable level whose anomalous surge of greed drove it to steal a Dungeon Core — a direct vessel for the God of Chaos, Maelora — from the depths of its sealed dungeon network. Glintjaw carried the core to the surface, where it became a violent magical siphon that triggered The Catastrophe: a 300-megaton arcane detonation that killed ninety percent of the global population, vaporized the ancient capital, and gave birth to the Adventurer System. No historian can explain why a bottom-tier dungeon creature would attempt such a thing. The name "Glintjaw" was assigned posthumously by the 15 Original Magi, who found the creature's calcified remains fused into the bedrock at the edge of The Maw.
